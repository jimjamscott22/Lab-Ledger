PC Won’t Wake From Sleep After a Hardware Upgrade

Track: Windows troubleshooting · REAL-WORLD FIX on your own Lenovo Legion Tower 5i Gen 8
⏱ 25–30 min (diagnostics + first pass); hardware phases extra, spread over the day Level: Intermediate Skill: powercfg, Get-WinEvent, drivers, BIOS, PSU/hardware isolation

Safety & warranty (read first): This is your real PC, not a VM. Every BIOS, firmware and hardware step is at your own risk. Before opening the case: shut down, flip the PSU switch off, unplug the power cord, then hold the power button ~10 s to drain residual charge. Ground yourself (ESD strap or touch bare chassis metal) and work on a non-carpeted surface. Never open the PSU itself. Opening a Lenovo OEM tower and swapping the PSU/GPU can affect warranty coverage; check your Lenovo warranty status and terms first. Don’t flash BIOS during a storm or on flaky power. Nothing in this lab has been tested on your hardware; it is a methodical process, not a guaranteed fix.

The ticket

INC-1107 · Priority: P3
Subject: PC won’t wake from sleep after hardware upgrade
User: Jamie Scott · Asset: Lenovo Legion Tower 5i Gen 8 (Intel 13th-gen, Windows 11)
“Once my PC goes to sleep, nothing wakes it: keyboard, mouse, even the power button. It started right after I swapped the factory RTX 3060 Ti for an RTX 5070 and replaced the PSU with an ASUS TUF Gaming 750W Gold. I poked at it a bit, found nothing.”

The classic help-desk lesson: what changed? Two variables changed at once (GPU + PSU), which is exactly what makes this tricky. You’ll go in order of likelihood and cost: free evidence first, then software, then firmware, then hardware. One variable at a time, writing everything down.

Prerequisites

  • Admin PowerShell (Win+X → Terminal (Admin)).
  • A USB stick + another device for downloads in case the display driver gets weird.
  • Free tools: Display Driver Uninstaller (DDU) from Wagnardsoft’s official site, current NVIDIA driver, Lenovo Vantage / support.lenovo.com.
  • Your phone to photograph every BIOS screen before changing it.

Stopgap workaround (do this now)

While you test, keep the machine usable: either disable sleep or use Hibernate (S4 writes RAM to disk and powers off fully, so it doesn’t depend on standby power holding RAM).

# Never sleep on AC power (0 = never)
powercfg /change standby-timeout-ac 0
# Make sure hibernate is available and use Start > Power > Hibernate manually
powercfg /hibernate on

If Hibernate resumes fine but Sleep doesn’t, that’s a useful clue: it points at the S3/standby path (RAM self-refresh, 5VSB power, wake logic) rather than the whole system.

Phase 0: Record before you change

powercfg /getactivescheme
# copy the GUID it prints, then:
powercfg /export C:\power-backup.pow <GUID>
powercfg /query > C:\power-settings-before.txt
mkdir C:\sleeplab

Photograph every BIOS page you might touch (Phase 3). Start a notes file: what you changed, when, original value.

Phase 1: Gather evidence (free, ~10 min)

powercfg /a

Look for Standby (S3) vs Standby (S0 Low Power Idle) Network Connected/Disconnected (Modern Standby). Desktops like this usually use S3. Note also whether Hibernate and Hybrid Sleep are listed. If S3 is “not available” because “the system firmware does not support” or a driver (often display) blocks it, write that down.

powercfg /lastwake
powercfg /devicequery wake_armed
powercfg /waketimers
powercfg /requests

wake_armed should list your keyboard/mouse (HID devices). If it’s empty, input can’t wake it by design, though the power button should still work. /requests shows what’s blocking sleep (not our symptom, but good context).

powercfg /energy /output C:\sleeplab\energy.html /duration 60
powercfg /sleepstudy /output C:\sleeplab\sleepstudy.html   # mainly useful on Modern Standby

Open the HTML. Errors about USB suspend, PCI Express ASPM, or the display adapter are leads.

# Entered sleep (Kernel-Power 42) and resumed (Power-Troubleshooter 1 = wake source)
Get-WinEvent -FilterHashtable @{LogName='System'; ProviderName='Microsoft-Windows-Kernel-Power'; Id=42} -MaxEvents 10 |
  Format-Table TimeCreated, Message -Wrap
Get-WinEvent -FilterHashtable @{LogName='System'; ProviderName='Microsoft-Windows-Power-Troubleshooter'; Id=1} -MaxEvents 10 |
  Format-Table TimeCreated, Message -Wrap

# Kernel-Power 41 = system rebooted without cleanly shutting down
Get-WinEvent -FilterHashtable @{LogName='System'; ProviderName='Microsoft-Windows-Kernel-Power'; Id=41} -MaxEvents 10 |
  ForEach-Object {
    $x = [xml]$_.ToXml()
    [pscustomobject]@{
      Time      = $_.TimeCreated
      BugCheck  = ($x.Event.EventData.Data | Where-Object Name -eq 'BugcheckCode').'#text'
      SleepInProgress = ($x.Event.EventData.Data | Where-Object Name -eq 'SleepInProgress').'#text'
      PowerButton = ($x.Event.EventData.Data | Where-Object Name -eq 'PowerButtonTimestamp').'#text'
    }
  } | Format-Table -AutoSize

# Unexpected shutdown (6008) and BugCheck (1001)
Get-WinEvent -FilterHashtable @{LogName='System'; Id=6008} -MaxEvents 5 -ErrorAction SilentlyContinue
Get-WinEvent -FilterHashtable @{LogName='System'; ProviderName='Microsoft-Windows-WER-SystemErrorReporting'; Id=1001} -MaxEvents 5 -ErrorAction SilentlyContinue

How to read it: a 42 with no matching Power-Troubleshooter 1 afterwards, followed by a Kernel-Power 41 with BugcheckCode 0, means the PC never resumed: it hung (or lost standby power) and you force-powered it off. A nonzero bugcheck means a real BSOD during resume (note the code, e.g. a driver power-state failure, and check C:\Windows\Minidump). SleepInProgress nonzero suggests failure during the sleep transition itself.

Hard hang vs reboot: next time it fails, observe before touching it: fans/LEDs on (S3 normally = fans off, power LED pulsing/dim)? Fans spinning hard with black screen = hung resume (often GPU/driver). Completely dead, no LED = possibly lost standby power (PSU/5VSB suspect). Does it reboot by itself? Write it down; it decides which phase is most likely.

perfmon /rel

Red X entries for “Windows was not properly shut down” or hardware errors line up the timeline with the upgrade date.

Branch point: Hung resume with fans on and a black screen leans GPU driver/firmware (Phases 2–3). Totally dead, needs PSU switch cycling, or sleep never really engages leans PSU/standby power/compatibility (Phase 4).

Phase 2: Software & driver layer (free, ~15 min + reboots)

Leftover 3060 Ti driver bits plus a new GPU is a top suspect. The RTX 50-series (Blackwell) launch had widely reported black-screen and sleep/wake problems on some driver versions; NVIDIA has addressed several in later releases. Read the “Fixed” and “Open Issues” sections of the current NVIDIA release notes for sleep/resume/black-screen items before choosing a version.

  1. Download the latest NVIDIA driver for RTX 5070 (and DDU) first; disconnect the network so Windows Update doesn’t auto-install a driver mid-process.
  2. Boot to Safe Mode:
# Reboot into Advanced Startup (then Troubleshoot > Advanced > Startup Settings > 4 Safe Mode)
shutdown /r /o /t 0
  1. Run DDU → GPU → NVIDIA → “Clean and restart”.
  2. Install the NVIDIA driver (Custom → Clean installation). Reconnect network.
  3. Test sleep. If it still fails, repeat with a different version: the previous Game Ready release or the Studio driver. Log versions you tried.
# Check Windows build and pending updates
winver
Start-Process ms-settings:windowsupdate

Install all updates including Optional updates → driver updates. Then get Intel Chipset, Management Engine (ME) and Serial IO drivers from Lenovo support for your exact machine type (the MTM on the sticker, or Get-CimInstance Win32_ComputerSystemProduct). The Intel ME driver/firmware is involved in power-state handling, so a stale one matters.

# Fast Startup off (it's hybrid shutdown and can leave drivers in odd states)
reg add "HKLM\SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Control\Session Manager\Power" /v HiberbootEnabled /t REG_DWORD /d 0 /f
# Rebuild hiberfil (fixes corrupt hibernate/hybrid sleep state)
powercfg /hibernate off
powercfg /hibernate on
# Turn off Hybrid Sleep for the test (0 = off)
powercfg /setacvalueindex SCHEME_CURRENT SUB_SLEEP HYBRIDSLEEP 0
powercfg /setactive SCHEME_CURRENT
# USB selective suspend: off (0)
powercfg /setacvalueindex SCHEME_CURRENT 2a737441-1930-4402-8d77-b2bebba308a3 48e6b7a6-50f5-4782-a5d4-53bb8f07e226 0
# PCI Express > Link State Power Management: off (0)
powercfg /setacvalueindex SCHEME_CURRENT SUB_PCIEXPRESS ASPM 0
powercfg /setactive SCHEME_CURRENT
# Verify
powercfg /query SCHEME_CURRENT SUB_PCIEXPRESS ASPM

If a setting is hidden in the GUI, powercfg -attributes <SUB> <SETTING> -ATTRIB_HIDE unhides it.

# List devices that CAN wake, then arm keyboard/mouse by exact name
powercfg /devicequery wake_programmable
powercfg /deviceenablewake "HID Keyboard Device"
powercfg /deviceenablewake "HID-compliant mouse"
powercfg /devicequery wake_armed

Also in Device Manager → keyboard/mouse → Power Management: check “Allow this device to wake the computer”. Plug keyboard/mouse into rear motherboard USB ports (not a hub or front panel) for testing.

NVIDIA Control Panel → Manage 3D settings → Power management mode → Prefer maximum performance (as a test). In the NVIDIA app, check for the same setting under Graphics → Global. Note: this mostly affects idle clocks; it’s cheap to try, not a likely root cause.

Phase 3: Firmware layer (free, ~15–30 min)

Photograph BIOS screens first. Don’t interrupt a BIOS flash. Menu names below vary by BIOS version; verify what your BIOS actually offers rather than hunting for an exact label.

# Current BIOS version and machine type
Get-CimInstance Win32_BIOS | Select-Object SMBIOSBIOSVersion, ReleaseDate
Get-CimInstance Win32_ComputerSystemProduct | Select-Object Name, Version

Use Lenovo Vantage (or support.lenovo.com → your Legion Tower 5i Gen 8 machine type) and read the BIOS changelog for fixes mentioning sleep, S3, resume, or newer GPU compatibility. A BIOS newer than the factory one is the single best firmware fix for “new GPU, old platform”.

Load Optimized/Setup Defaults first (F2/F1 at boot, varies), test sleep. Then look for, and record, these as one-at-a-time tests:

  • ErP / EuP / Deep Sleep / Lite: when enabled it cuts standby power, which can disable USB wake. Try disabled for testing.
  • Sleep state (S3 vs Modern Standby/“OS-controlled”; some BIOSes label it “Linux S3”): if offered, compare against what powercfg /a showed.
  • PCIe ASPM / Native ASPM: try disabled.
  • Above 4G Decoding / Resizable BAR: toggle off as a test (RTX 50 cards work with or without ReBAR).
  • XMP / memory profile: disable as a test; aggressive memory settings can fail RAM self-refresh on resume.
  • USB wake / USB power in S3/S4 / Legacy USB: enable wake from USB for keyboard/mouse.

With power cord unplugged: use the board’s CLR_CMOS jumper (check the Lenovo hardware maintenance manual for your model) or remove the coin cell for a few minutes. Re-enter BIOS, set date/time, re-apply only what you need.

Phase 4: Hardware layer (prime suspect: power)

Power off, flip PSU switch off, unplug, hold power button 10 s, ground yourself. Never mix modular cables between PSUs: modular pinouts differ by brand and model, and a wrong cable can destroy components. Use only the cables that came in the ASUS TUF box.

Lenovo OEM towers often use non-standard motherboard power: some Legion/IdeaCentre boards use a proprietary 12-pin/14-pin style connector (ATX12VO-like, single-rail 12V, with standby power generated on the board) instead of a standard ATX 24-pin, plus their own front-panel/power-button headers. A retail ATX PSU then needs a specific adapter.

  • Look at the motherboard’s main power connector: is it a standard 24-pin ATX, or a smaller proprietary one? Same for the CPU (EPS 8-pin vs other).
  • If you used an adapter, identify it exactly (brand, which pins). Cheap adapters may omit or mis-wire the 5VSB / PS_ON / Power-Good lines.
  • Why this matters: in S3, nearly everything is off except RAM self-refresh and wake logic, which run on +5V standby (5VSB) (or on-board-generated standby on 12V-only designs). If standby power is missing, weak, or mis-wired, the PC can enter sleep and then can’t hold RAM or see the wake signal, so it looks “dead”. A PSU/motherboard compatibility issue is a top suspect given the timing.
  • Check Lenovo’s documentation and Lenovo/ASUS support or community threads for your exact machine type plus retail PSU swaps before going further.

RTX 5070: use the PSU’s native 12V-2x6 / 12VHPWR cable if it has one, or the adapter that shipped with the GPU fed by separate PCIe 8-pin cables (one cable per input, no daisy-chained pigtails, no third-party splitters). Push until fully seated with no gap; the 12V-2x6 connector must click and sit flush, with no sharp bend right at the plug.

  • Reseat GPU fully in the x16 slot; check the slot latch and bracket screw.
  • Reseat RAM; test with one stick in the slot the manual recommends, then the other.
  • Unplug non-essential USB devices, RGB controllers, and hubs; test sleep.
# Does your CPU have an iGPU? (13th-gen "F" models don't)
Get-CimInstance Win32_Processor | Select-Object Name
Get-CimInstance Win32_VideoController | Select-Object Name, DriverVersion

If the CPU isn’t an “F” model and your motherboard has its own video out (many Legion towers don’t expose one, so check the rear I/O), remove the RTX 5070, connect the monitor to the motherboard, and test sleep. Works without the GPU → GPU/driver/GPU-power issue. Still fails → PSU/board/firmware.

Best test: temporarily reinstall the original Lenovo PSU with its own cables (if it can power the 5070 at all, test briefly at idle) or use the original PSU with the old 3060 Ti. If sleep works on the OEM PSU, the ASUS PSU/adapter combo is your answer. Alternative: a known-good PSU from a friend/shop, same caveats on connectors.

Keep receipts. ASUS TUF PSU and the RTX 5070 both have manufacturer warranties; retailers often have easier return windows. If the root cause is incompatibility (not a defect), a return or a Lenovo-compatible PSU/adapter solution is the fix, not an RMA. Contact Lenovo support if the board itself is suspect (expect questions about the non-OEM parts).

Decision tree

  • Does Hibernate (S4) resume OK?
    • No → broader issue: Windows/driver/firmware; do Phases 2–3 fully, check bugchecks.
    • Yes → S3-specific. Continue.
  • When it fails, is the PC hung with fans on (black screen) or dead (no lights, needs PSU switch)?
    • Hung, fans on → GPU driver/firmware first: DDU, alternate driver, BIOS update, ReBAR/ASPM toggles → iGPU test.
    • Dead / needs power cycle → standby power: PSU adapter/24-pin compatibility, ErP setting, cabling → PSU swap test.
  • Does it work with the GPU removed (iGPU)? Yes → GPU/GPU power. No → PSU/board/firmware.
  • Does it work with the original PSU? Yes → ASUS PSU/adapter incompatibility → return/replace or correct adapter. No → board/firmware → Lenovo support.
  • Nothing fixes it? Keep sleep disabled / use Hibernate and escalate.

When to escalate / RMA

  • Any burning smell, melted/discolored 12V-2x6 connector, or PSU clicking: stop, unplug, RMA.
  • Fails identically with GPU removed and a known-good PSU: likely motherboard/firmware → Lenovo support.
  • Works only with original PSU: return the ASUS within its window or source a Lenovo-compatible PSU.
  • BSODs with consistent bugcheck codes after clean drivers: collect minidumps, report to NVIDIA/Lenovo.

Verify it worked

# Sleep now (if hibernate is on this may hibernate; use Start > Power > Sleep instead)
# After each wake:
powercfg /lastwake
Get-WinEvent -FilterHashtable @{LogName='System'; ProviderName='Microsoft-Windows-Kernel-Power'; Id=42,107,41} -MaxEvents 10 |
  Format-Table TimeCreated, Id, Message -Wrap
Get-WinEvent -FilterHashtable @{LogName='System'; ProviderName='Microsoft-Windows-Power-Troubleshooter'; Id=1} -MaxEvents 3 |
  Format-List TimeCreated, Message

Pass criteria: at least 3 sleeps of 10+ minutes (and one overnight), waking once each by keyboard, mouse, and power button; each 42 followed by 107 (resume) and a Power-Troubleshooter 1 with the right wake source; no new Kernel-Power 41. Then re-enable your normal sleep timer.

Cleanup / rollback

# Restore the original scheme (gets a new GUID), then activate it
powercfg /import C:\power-backup.pow
powercfg /list
powercfg /setactive <GUID-from-list>
# Restore a sleep timer (minutes)

Keep only the change(s) that fixed it. Put back BIOS values from your photos (XMP back on, ReBAR back on, etc.) one at a time, re-testing sleep after each. Re-enable Fast Startup only if you want it.

Interview questions

A user's PC won't wake from sleep. Walk me through your troubleshooting.

Show answer

Ask what changed (hardware, drivers, updates). Gather evidence: powercfg /a, /lastwake, /devicequery wake_armed, Event Viewer (Kernel-Power 42/41, Power-Troubleshooter 1). Apply a stopgap (hibernate or disable sleep). Then go cheapest-first: drivers (clean GPU reinstall, chipset), power settings (Fast Startup, USB suspend, ASPM), BIOS update and settings, then hardware isolation (cabling, RAM, GPU, PSU). Change one variable at a time, document, verify with multiple sleep cycles, escalate if hardware is at fault.

What do S0, S3, S4, and S5 mean?

Show answer

ACPI power states. S0 = working (Modern Standby is S0 low-power idle). S3 = sleep/suspend-to-RAM: RAM stays powered on standby power, fast resume. S4 = hibernate: RAM saved to hiberfil.sys, power off. S5 = soft off (shutdown); only standby power for the power button/wake.

How do you isolate a hardware fault?

Show answer

Reproduce reliably, then change one variable at a time against a baseline: minimal config (one RAM stick, no extra devices), swap in known-good parts (PSU, GPU or iGPU), and move components between slots. If the fault follows the part, the part is bad; if it stays with the system, look at the board, firmware, or compatibility. Log every test.

Stretch goal: one-shot power diagnostics report

Write a script that dumps everything into one folder so you (or a future help-desk tech) can attach it to a ticket. Starter:

# Save as Get-PowerDiag.ps1 and run from an elevated PowerShell
$out = "C:\sleeplab\diag-$(Get-Date -Format yyyyMMdd-HHmm)"
New-Item -ItemType Directory -Path $out -Force | Out-Null
powercfg /a                         > "$out\states.txt"
powercfg /lastwake                  > "$out\lastwake.txt"
powercfg /devicequery wake_armed    > "$out\wake_armed.txt"
powercfg /requests                  > "$out\requests.txt"
powercfg /query                     > "$out\scheme.txt"
powercfg /energy /output "$out\energy.html" /duration 30 | Out-Null
Get-CimInstance Win32_BIOS | Format-List * > "$out\bios.txt"
Get-CimInstance Win32_VideoController | Select-Object Name, DriverVersion, DriverDate > "$out\gpu.txt"
Get-WinEvent -FilterHashtable @{LogName='System'; Id=41,42,107,1,6008,1001} -MaxEvents 200 -ErrorAction SilentlyContinue |
  Select-Object TimeCreated, ProviderName, Id, Message | Export-Csv "$out\power-events.csv" -NoTypeInformation
Compress-Archive -Path "$out\*" -DestinationPath "$out.zip" -Force
Write-Host "Report: $out.zip"

Extra nerd points: parse Kernel-Power 41 XML for BugcheckCode and add a summary line at the top; add a -Since parameter.
